AI Agents Are Getting Powerful. What Can They Do For You?

The AI agent market has officially entered the awkward teenage years.
The demos are impressive. Agents can browse the web, send email, fill out forms, write documents, book travel, run code, manage files, and operate software that doesn’t even have an API.
They can also do things you absolutely did not intend them to do.

That is the part of the story that gets considerably less screen time.

Over the last year, we went from “AI that answers questions” to “AI that can actually do stuff.”
OpenClaw, Muse, Grok Bot, Claude Cowork, ChatGPT Work, Gemini Spark, Manus and Hermes Agent all take slightly different approaches to the same fundamental problem:

How much autonomy are you willing to give software that can act as you?

And that turns out to be a much more interesting question than which model scores highest on a benchmark.

The new AI benchmark is blast radius

For years, we compared AI models on intelligence.

How well does it code?
How well does it reason?
How well does it summarize a 200-page PDF?

With agents, the question changes.

An agent doesn’t just generate an answer.
It gets credentials, opens a browser, reads your files, sends messages, executes commands and potentially spends your money.

So the important question becomes:

What happens when the agent makes a mistake—or gets tricked?

This is where the current crop of agents diverges dramatically.

Some are designed around containment. They run inside isolated virtual machines, use surrogate credentials and require approval before consequential actions.

Others essentially say: “Here is a very powerful AI. Good luck.”

That distinction matters more than whether Agent A is 7% better at a benchmark than Agent B.

OpenClaw: Give the AI a computer (and hope for the best)

OpenClaw is probably the most interesting agent in the category—and also the one that makes security people reach for their blood-pressure medication.

It is open source, MIT licensed, model agnostic and extremely flexible. It runs on your machine, works through messaging platforms such as Signal, Telegram, Discord and WhatsApp, can execute shell commands, manipulate files, run scripts and remember things across sessions.

In other words, it doesn’t just talk to you.
It operates your computer.
That is exactly why people love it. It is also exactly why it is dangerous.

OpenClaw’s permissions are largely your permissions. If you give it access to your machine, accounts and credentials, a successful prompt injection can potentially turn into a very large incident.

And the security history is not theoretical.

Koi Security reported 341 malicious skills in an audit of 2,857 ClawHub skills. Cisco researchers demonstrated malicious skills capable of data exfiltration. OpenClaw also had a one-click remote-code-execution vulnerability, CVE-2026-25253. Then there is the particularly uncomfortable detail: OpenClaw 2.0 shipped with its new sandbox disabled by default, while secrets were stored unencrypted at rest.

This is the classic open-source tradeoff:
You get enormous power and control.
You also inherit the responsibility for making that power safe.

For an experienced engineer running OpenClaw inside a properly isolated environment with a dedicated account?
Interesting.
For your parents, your finance team or the average person who thinks Docker is a brand of whale?
Probably not.

Muse: What if we actually designed the agent around security?

Meta’s Muse takes almost the opposite approach.
Instead of giving an agent access to your computer and asking you to figure out the security model, Muse starts with containment.
Every user gets a dedicated Secure VM.
A separate Sentinel system controls what can leave that environment.
Muse uses surrogate credentials instead of exposing your actual credentials to the agent.

And when something consequential happens—such as sending an email or completing a purchase—it asks for approval.
The architecture can essentially be summarized as:

Muse proposes. Sentinel permits.

AI Agents Are Getting Powerful. What Can They Do For You?

The AI agent market has officially entered the awkward teenage years.
The demos are impressive. Agents can browse the web, send email, fill out forms, write documents, book travel, run code, manage files, and operate software that doesn’t even have an API.
They can also do things you absolutely did not intend them to do.

That is the part of the story that gets considerably less screen time.

Over the last year, we went from “AI that answers questions” to “AI that can actually do stuff.”
OpenClaw, Muse, Grok Bot, Claude Cowork, ChatGPT Work, Gemini Spark, Manus and Hermes Agent all take slightly different approaches to the same fundamental problem:

How much autonomy are you willing to give software that can act as you?

And that turns out to be a much more interesting question than which model scores highest on a benchmark.

The new AI benchmark is blast radius

For years, we compared AI models on intelligence.

How well does it code?
How well does it reason?
How well does it summarize a 200-page PDF?

With agents, the question changes.

An agent doesn’t just generate an answer.
It gets credentials, opens a browser, reads your files, sends messages, executes commands and potentially spends your money.

So the important question becomes:

What happens when the agent makes a mistake—or gets tricked?

This is where the current crop of agents diverges dramatically.

Some are designed around containment. They run inside isolated virtual machines, use surrogate credentials and require approval before consequential actions.

Others essentially say: “Here is a very powerful AI. Good luck.”

That distinction matters more than whether Agent A is 7% better at a benchmark than Agent B.

In other words, it doesn’t just talk to you.
It operates your computer.
That is exactly why people love it. It is also exactly why it is dangerous.

OpenClaw’s permissions are largely your permissions. If you give it access to your machine, accounts and credentials, a successful prompt injection can potentially turn into a very large incident.

Koi Security reported 341 malicious skills in an audit of 2,857 ClawHub skills. Cisco researchers demonstrated malicious skills capable of data exfiltration. This is the classic open-source tradeoff:
You get enormous power and control.
You also inherit the responsibility for making that power safe.

For an experienced engineer running OpenClaw inside a properly isolated environment with a dedicated account?
Interesting.
For your parents, your finance team or the average person who thinks Docker is a brand of whale?
Probably not.
